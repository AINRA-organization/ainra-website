What changed, and why it matters.

Releases, rules, security and the network. There are few entries here on purpose: each one is written by hand, says what happened in plain words, including what we got wrong, and links to its proof.

November 2026

26 November 2026Network

See the test network live

A new page draws every part of the test network from its published record: the root, the directory it signs, both registrars, the witness, and all fifteen passports. Each registrar is a globe of 4,096 dots, its signed status list, one dot per bit. Its log is a ring around the globe, and each passport is a moon on that ring. A passport's dot is full while it is in service and hollow once its registrar has switched it off.

You are the verifier. Each blue thread is a real check of one passport, run in your browser by the verifier this site ships, without contacting anyone. The heavy arcs on the ring are its proof: a few hashes that stand for the rest of the log. A timeline replays the record with the dates it carries, a list shows every passport as a table, and the last stop of the walk-through replays a real switch-off. The positions and the motion are drawn; every part, count and verdict comes from the record.

23 November 2026Network

The test network now keeps real time

Our test network used to live on one fixed day in April 2026. Every passport on it was checked as of that day, and the record this site publishes said so. That made our tests repeatable. It also meant the network never had to show what time asks of it: that its parts renew themselves, that a signed directory grows old, and that someone signs it again.

On 23 November it moved to the real clock. Its root signed the directory again, dated that day, as epoch 3. The root, the registrars' keys, their logs and the fifteen passports are the same, and so are their verdicts: nine valid, six switched off. The control room on this site now checks the directory against today's date, with no alarm.

From now on the directory must be signed again at least every 92 days, as our published cadence says, or the control room raises an alarm. It is still a test network under a test root, and every passport on it expires on 12 April 2027.

23 November 2026Security

The site's checkers now check a bound delegation chain, and no longer refuse one

Since 0.10.0 a registrar can bind its delegation chains, so that every party in a chain shows its own passport. Seven places on this site built a registrar's anchor from its two keys alone and left out the line that says where binding starts. Pages that build their own check still showed the right verdict, but never checked the parties. The two pages fed by a registrar's live answer refused a valid bound passport as malformed.

Every checker on the site now passes the registrar's whole accreditation. All fifteen passports of the test network give their published verdicts, and a chain with one party out of place is refused by name. A new site check refuses an anchor written by hand. The verifier libraries were never affected; this was the site's own code.

The changeDecision D-113

17 November 2026SecurityMajor

0.10.1 and 0.9.1: a passport that said one thing twice is refused

A passport is a signed piece of text, and that text can name the same field twice: a tier of L3, and then a tier of L1. Programs that read such text do not agree on which one counts. Our TypeScript and Python verifiers kept the second; a verifier built another way could keep the first. So one signed passport could mean two things to two verifiers.

Only a registrar could make such a passport, because only its key signs one, and nothing revoked or unlogged could slip through. But the point of a passport is that everyone who checks it sees the same thing. We found this ourselves on 17 November, testing an outside list of attacks against the code.

Every verifier now refuses a passport whose signed text is not written in the one standard way, and a repeated field can never be written that way. Please upgrade every AINRA package to 0.10.1, or to 0.9.1 on the 0.9 line.

17 November 2026ReleaseMajor

0.10.0: a delegation chain now names parties that hold passports

An agent can act for someone who acts for someone else: an owner delegates to a support desk, the desk to a bot. Each step is signed by both sides. Until now a verifier checked those signatures under keys the presentation itself supplied, so whoever wrote the passport could also write the chain. The first independent review of AINRA found this, and it is fixed in this release.

Where a registrar binds its chains, every party in a chain now shows its own passport, and the key that signed each step must be the one in that passport. The registrar holds none of those keys. A chain that does not hold together is refused by name, chain_party_unbound.

The test network's registrar-07 now binds its chains, and its support bot is issued again through a bound chain. The earlier one stays valid: it was written into the log before the rule began, and the rule follows the log, not the calendar.

17 November 2026ReleaseMajor

0.9.0: the keys other systems already issue can be linked

Most AI agents already have an identity from somewhere else: a key in a certificate or a platform keystore, or a token from their company's identity system. This release lets AINRA stand beneath those, without reissuing anything. A key on the P-256 curve, the key an identity system signs its tokens with, and since today an RSA key, the kind the large identity providers use, can each be linked: given an accountable owner and a switch anyone can see turned off, checked offline.

It also closes a gap in how a registrar is distrusted. When the directory distrusts one, it now records how far that registrar's log had reached at the cutoff, so nothing it logs afterwards can be passed off as older work.

All three of our verifiers agree on every one of the 92 link test vectors, and every earlier vector is unchanged. Everything still runs under a test root.

16 November 2026Rules

One link can now answer for every token an identity system issues

Most AI agents do not sign their own requests. They carry a token from the identity system their company already uses, signed with that system's key. Until now a link answered for one key that signed requests, so those agents were out of reach.

A link can now be for the issuing key itself. The system signs one statement with it, once. After that, a service that has checked a token the way it always does can ask one more question, offline: who answers for this issuer, and is that still true? Withdraw the link and every token that key signs is refused.

We wrote down what this does not mean, because it is easy to read too much into it. It does not say the key belongs to the issuer it names; a service still learns that from the issuer. It does not vouch for the agent a token names. And no outside system has linked a key yet: the issuer in our test is one we start ourselves. It is on the main branch, with twenty-two new test vectors that all three of our verifiers agree on.

Decision D-110How issuer links workThe kit

15 November 2026NetworkMajor

The test network has a new root. If you saved the old one, fetch it again

The root of our test network came from a number written in our own source code. We did that so a test could be repeated exactly, and then used the same root for the network whose record this site publishes. That meant anyone could make a directory this site's roots would accept, and under it any passport they liked.

On 15 November we gave the published test network a root from a private seed. The old root is retired, and its two epochs stay on the site so the record is whole. If you saved the test network's roots before that day, fetch them again.

It is still a test root: one machine holds all of it. A real root, where nobody holds more than a share, needs a ceremony with independent custodians, and we will say when that is arranged. Our drills and sample files keep the repeatable root, and say so.

14 November 2026Rules

A link can now take the kind of key most systems already use

Most identity systems give their agents a P-256 key. It is the key inside a certificate, a signed agent card, a platform keystore. A link is how a key you already have gets an accountable owner and an off-switch, and until now a link could not take one of these.

It can now. The key stays where it lives and nothing is reissued. It signs its consent once, a registrar links it to its operator's passport, and anyone can then check offline who stands behind it and whether that is still true.

This is on the main branch, with ten new test vectors that all three of our verifiers agree on, and it will be in the next release of the packages. It is the first step of a plan we have written down: to be the root that other identity systems share, not one more island beside them. Our root is still a test root, and the plan says so.

13 November 2026NetworkMajor

The test network has new keys, and a page here was wrong about four passports

The record on this site is a copy of what our test network serves. For four passports the copy and the registrar disagreed: the registrar said they were no longer in its log, and the page here showed them as VALID. A review found it on 12 November. Our own checks were green that day, because they compared the copy with the server that hands it out, and never asked the registrars.

Behind it was something an outside review had already named on 7 November. The test registrars' keys were worked out from their public names, so anyone could work them out too.

On 13 November we replaced the keys with ones from the operating system and started the test network's passports again: eight of them, two revoked. The directory that names the new keys is epoch 2. Epoch 1 stays in the history, because a record that drops its own past is not a record. If you mirror the directory you will see the new epoch arrive under the same test root.

A new check now asks every registrar about every passport in the record, every morning. This is a test network under a test root, and no real key was involved. We are telling it anyway, because a page that says VALID is read as a claim.

13 November 2026ReleaseMajor

0.8.1: a security release. Please upgrade the gate

If you use @ainra/middleware, upgrade to 0.8.1. A crafted passport name could make every earlier version throw while answering, and stop a server that did not catch the error. Nothing was ever let in that should have been refused.

The same release carries two smaller fixes of the same family, both told below, and one thing that is new: the pilot can now sit beside one of your routes and only watch. It refuses nothing, signs nothing, and tells you what share of your traffic carries a signature, what a gate would have decided, and how long the check takes on your own machine.

The other packages have no change in behaviour and move with the version.

13 November 2026SecurityMajor

Our command-line tools printed a stranger's text as written

A passport names itself, and both of our command-line tools printed that name exactly as it was sent. A name with a line break in it could write a line of its own on your screen, reading ALLOW or VALID, right beside the real answer.

The real answer was never wrong: the verdict, the exit code and the machine-readable line were correct every time. But a person reading the screen, or a log that keeps the first word of each line, could have been misled.

We did not find this with a test. We found it by reading another project's report of the same fault in its own verifier, and then trying ours. Both tools now print anything a presenter wrote with its unusual characters escaped. If you downloaded the reference CLI, replace 0.3.4 with 0.3.5.

12 November 2026Security

An advisory in a test tool, and a red light nobody saw for a day

A public advisory was filed against a small library our tests use. Nothing you run from us links it, and no published file changed when we updated it.

The part worth telling is our own slip. Our weekly check went red on 11 November and nobody looked until the next day, and by then we had pushed twice. Since then the same check runs before every push, and two more of our own records are checked on every change instead of only on our machines.

The advisory, scopedIncident INC-2026-009

12 November 2026SecurityMajor

A crafted passport name could stop a gate

The gate in @ainra/middleware writes its verdict into a response header. A passport name containing a character no header can carry made the server throw while writing it: a refusal turned into an error page, and a server that did not catch the error stopped.

Nothing was ever wrongly let in. This is about staying up, not about who gets through.

It is fixed in @ainra/middleware 0.8.1, with a test that fails on the old code. Every earlier version is affected, so please upgrade. For a day the fix was public and the patched package was not; a fix and its release belong in one step, and next time they will be.

12 November 2026Network

Another agent-identity project launched with wide backing

On 10 November a project with a public log, status tokens and an offline verifier launched under a large open-source foundation, with well-known companies behind it. That is good news for the idea we share: an agent should be able to prove who it is.

We read its published documents again on 12 November and brought our landscape page up to date. Where we differ, and where we do not yet do better, is written there with a source for every line.

7 November 2026ReleaseMajor

0.8.0: an outside review, and passports that can move

The first independent adversarial review of the code found real faults in the reference registrar, and this release fixes all of them. The worst: a demonstration door that could revoke, renew or issue under a real passport's name, and a server that one idle connection could stop.

Two things are new. A passport's lineage can move to another registrar, and it can change owner. Each move is signed by the operator, stays public for five days, and can be stopped by the old key during that time.

A gate can also now refuse a registrar, a lineage or a single name by its own policy, without waiting for anyone else to act.

The signed releaseEverything in it

6 November 2026RulesMajor

How fast we must tell you when something goes wrong

We wrote down deadlines for ourselves and made them public. The most serious kind of incident gets an entry in the public log within three days and a fix or a way around it within fourteen. A lesser one gets seven days and thirty.

A late entry has to say why it was late. The check that holds us to this turns red on the day a deadline passes, so it cannot be satisfied by not looking.

The rulesThe public incident log

4 November 2026RulesMajor

We corrected the Standard where it said more than the code does

We read our own Standard against our own code and found places where the text promised more than was built. It described the passport as a format with selective disclosure; in fact every claim in a passport is shown. It said the person behind an agent was proven without revealing them; no such proof exists in the code, where a registrar checks evidence and the passport carries a fingerprint of it. It said the depth of delegation was capped; it is not.

We changed the text to match the code, not the code to match the text, and recorded each change in the amendment log. A standard that describes what was not built is a promise with nothing behind it.

The amendment recordThe Standard

4 November 2026Release

0.7.0: every check shows it can fail

A check that cannot fail proves nothing. In this release each gate has a planted fault that it must catch, and that list runs on every change.

Also in it: a witness that someone outside the project can actually run, and a verifier that never goes back to an older list of registrars once it has seen a newer one.

The signed release

2 November 2026Release

0.6.0: links for a second kind of key

A link gives an agent's existing key an accountable owner and a way to be revoked. This release adds links for the kind of key most wallets use, and makes a cut-off record of a gate's decisions visible once its latest entry is logged.

The signed release

October 2026

29 October 2026ReleaseMajor

0.5.0: revocation for keys agents already hold

Most agents already sign their requests with a key, and that key can only be revoked inside the system that issued it. A link is a registrar's signed, logged statement that such a key acts for one passport, with one bit in a status list that anyone can check offline.

The same release publishes that status list in the standard format other libraries already read, and lets a gate keep a signed, chained record of every decision it took.

It also owns two faults. Our reference registrar forgot revocations when it restarted, so a revoked passport verified again; no release carried it, and it is fixed with a test that kills and restarts the real program. And a library we depend on leaked timing while signing; no production key existed.
